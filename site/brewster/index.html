The rules of the game

$

The will's terms are short, and each one closes off an easy way to spend. Here they are, paraphrased from the film's plot summary. Try planning the month yourself, then open How it gets tricky further down.

  1. Spend $30 million in 30 days.
  2. Own nothing at the end that wasn't already his.
  3. Get value for the services of anyone he hires.
  4. Don't damage or destroy anything valuable he buys.
  5. Give up to 5% to charity and lose up to 5% gambling, $1.5 million each. Give none of it away otherwise.
  6. Tell no one about the challenge.
  7. Or take $1 million up front and walk away.

Backed by the plot

  1. Start from the will

    The tab must end at exactly $30 million, with nothing left to own.

  2. Price what's on screen

    Each spend is a formula in the ledger, like 40 staff at $900 a day for 28 days. Unit prices are unsourced guesses for 1985.

  3. Charge running costs daily

    Wages, rent, the suite and the cars accrue from when they start until the deadline, one story day per 2.6 minutes of film.

  4. Subtract money that comes back

    Deals that pay off by accident go in as negative amounts, so the line dips.

  5. Keep the gap in one entry

    The the estimates can't explain sits in one entry, marked as a plug.

  6. Check against the dialogue

    A script lines events up with a subtitle file, so prices said aloud can replace guesses.

How it gets tricky with examples…

$

Buying things backfires

Anything he buys is still his on day 30, and selling it brings money back. So the money has to go on things that disappear: rent, wages, services, meals and events.

In the tab: almost every entry is a rental, a wage, a service or an event.

Paying ahead counts as owning

Pay a year's rent up front and he's holding eleven months of prepaid rent, which an accountant would list as an asset. Deposits and refundable tickets have the same problem.

In the tab: running costs are charged by the day, not up front.

Losing on purpose is hard

Gambling losses stop counting at $1.5 million, and every win adds to the pile. Long-shot investments look like a way out, but spread enough of them and one comes in. If ten long shots each have a 1-in-10 chance of paying off, the chance that at least one does is about 65%.

In the tab: $1M of bad bets, under the $1.5M cap.

Help makes it worse

Spike doesn't know the rules, so he does what a good friend would: he invests Monty's money well. Every good deal puts cash back in Monty's hands.

In the tab: Spike's deals bring back .

The money earns money

Thirty million dollars in a bank account earns interest while it waits. At 8% a year, an assumption for 1985, that's about $6,600 a day and roughly $197,000 over the month, which also has to go.

In the tab: not modelled. It would add to the plug too.

A million a day

Thirty million in thirty days is $1 million a day, $41,667 an hour, or $11.57 a second, through every night he sleeps. Few purchases move money that fast, so a handful of big items carry most of the load.

Midnight means paid

A bill that hasn't been paid by the deadline is cash still sitting in his account. On the last day, every payment has to clear before the clock runs out.

In the tab: the last goes out at .

A paycheck is an asset

Winning the mayor's race would pay $60,000 a year, and a salary he's owed counts as an asset under the will. Being popular becomes a problem.

In the tab: he quits the race at 81:00 while leading.

Walking away is a bet too

The wimp clause pays $1 million for certain. Going for $300 million is worse on paper only if his chance of pulling it off is below 1 in 300.

In the tab: he goes for it at 17:30.
