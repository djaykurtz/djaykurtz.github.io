A thought exercise

The rules of the game

The will's terms are short, and each one closes off an easy way to spend. Here they are, paraphrased from the film; the exact wording gets checked along with the prices. Try planning the month yourself before reading where they get tricky.

  1. Spend $30 million in 30 days.
  2. Own nothing at the end. Whatever he buys has to be used up or gone.
  3. Give no more than 5% to charity and lose no more than 5% gambling. That's $1.5 million each.
  4. Don't destroy anything of value just to get rid of it.
  5. Tell no one why.
  6. Or skip all of it and take $1 million now.

Where it gets tricky

Buying things backfires

Anything he buys is still his on day 30, and selling it brings money back. So the money has to go on things that disappear: rent, wages, services, meals and events.

In the tab: almost every entry is a rental, a wage, a service or an event.

Paying ahead counts as owning

Pay a year's rent up front and he's holding eleven months of prepaid rent, which an accountant would list as an asset. Deposits and refundable tickets have the same problem.

In the tab: running costs are charged by the day, not up front.

Losing on purpose is hard

Gambling losses stop counting at $1.5 million, and every win adds to the pile. Long-shot investments look like a way out, but spread enough of them and one comes in. If ten long shots each have a 1-in-10 chance of paying off, the chance that at least one does is about 65%.

In the tab: two investments pay off and comes back.

Help makes it worse

He can't explain himself, so anyone trying to help him would try to save him money. A negotiated discount, a returned deposit or a smart tip on a stock all put cash back in his hands.

In the tab: not modelled. Every favour would add to the plug.

The money earns money

Thirty million dollars in a bank account earns interest while it waits. At 8% a year, an assumption for 1985, that's about $6,600 a day and roughly $197,000 over the month, which also has to go.

In the tab: not modelled. It would add to the plug too.

A million a day

Thirty million in thirty days is $1 million a day, $41,667 an hour, or $11.57 a second, through every night he sleeps. Few purchases move money that fast, so a handful of big items carry most of the load.

Midnight means paid

A bill that hasn't been paid by the deadline is cash still sitting in his account. On the last day, every payment has to clear before the clock runs out.

Walking away is a bet too

The wimp clause pays $1 million for certain. Going for $300 million is worse on paper only if his chance of pulling it off is below 1 in 300.

In the tab: he goes for it at 17:30.

How the numbers work

The film fixes two numbers: $30 million to spend and 30 days to spend it. It shows plenty of spending but names few prices. So every amount here is a small model, a quantity times a unit price in 1985 dollars, with a low and a high guess around it.

Prices from the dialogue

Waiting on the subtitle check

  1. Start from the will's terms

    The tab has to end at exactly $30,000,000, and Monty can't own anything when the month is up. That rules out buying things to keep, so most of the money goes on rent, wages, services and things that get used up. Charity and gambling losses are each capped at 5%, or $1.5 million.

  2. Price what's on screen

    Each spend gets a formula you can check in the ledger, like 40 staff at $900 a day for 28 days. The unit prices are unsourced assumptions about what things cost in New York in 1985.

  3. Spread running costs over the month

    Salaries, rent, the suite, the cars and the guards are promised by the day, so they're charged by the day from when they start until the deadline. Campaign staff stop when he drops out. To place days on screen, the story's 30 days are spread evenly over the film from 20:30 to 99:30, one day about every 2.6 minutes. That's why the line climbs between big purchases.

  4. Subtract money that comes back

    When a deal picked to lose money pays off, it goes in as a negative amount. That's why the line dips, and it means Monty has to spend that money a second time.

  5. Let one entry balance the books

    The itemized estimates leave unaccounted for. That gap goes into one entry, "Settling every bill," marked as a plug. It's the least supported number on the page.

  6. Check it against the dialogue

    For now, events are placed by story order. A small script reads a subtitle file, moves each event to the line where it happens, and lists every line that mentions money so prices said aloud can replace estimates.
